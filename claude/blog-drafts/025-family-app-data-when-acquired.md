# What Happens to Your Family's Data When an App Is Acquired

You chose a family app. You read enough of the privacy policy to feel
comfortable. You put your children's schedules, schools and photographs in it.

Then the company is sold, and the people you chose are no longer the people
holding the data.

This is not hypothetical in this category — family and calendar apps are bought
and folded into larger products regularly. Here is what actually governs it,
what the policies really say, and the one line worth looking for before you
commit.

*General information, not legal advice.*

## The clause is already in the policy you agreed to

Almost every consumer app has one. Here is what three family apps actually say.

**Cozi** — the long version, which also covers bankruptcy:

> "We may disclose Personal Information as part of a corporate business
> transaction, such as a merger or acquisition, joint venture, corporate
> reorganization, financing, or sale of company assets … We may also disclose
> Personal Information in the event of insolvency, bankruptcy, or receivership
> in which Personal Information could be transferred to third parties as one of
> the business assets in the transaction."

**OurFamilyWizard** — one line inside a list:

> "in connection with any possible sale merger or restructuring of all or any
> part of our business or assets"

**TimeTree** — the shortest of all:

> "Information may be provided in the event of a merger or other business
> succession."

Notice what none of the three contains: **a promise to tell you.**

For contrast, Google's policy does make that promise — that it will "give
affected users notice before personal information is transferred or becomes
subject to a different privacy policy."

That difference is the single most useful thing in this article. The clause
itself is normal and nearly universal. Whether it comes with a commitment to
notify you first is a genuine difference between companies, and it takes thirty
seconds to check.

## What the law actually requires

There is no GDPR article about mergers. The words do not appear as a governing
provision, because an acquisition is treated as an ordinary disclosure of
personal data to a new controller — so the general rules apply.

The lawful basis is usually **legitimate interests, Article 6(1)(f)**. What
makes that interesting is **Recital 47**, which turns on what you would expect:

> "The legitimate interests of a controller … may provide a legal basis for
> processing, provided that the interests or the fundamental rights and freedoms
> of the data subject are not overriding, taking into consideration the
> reasonable expectations of data subjects based on their relationship with the
> controller."

And it adds that those interests may be overridden "where personal data are
processed in circumstances where data subjects do not reasonably expect further
processing."

In plain words: what you reasonably expected when you handed the data over
matters legally, not just morally.

On telling you, European data protection authorities have endorsed guidance that
is unusually direct. Changes that should **always** be communicated include "a
change to the identity of the controller" — and that information "should be
provided to the data subject well in advance of the change actually taking
effect", by a method that is "explicit and effective".

A change of owner is a change of controller. So under EU guidance, being told —
in advance, clearly — is the expectation, whatever an individual privacy policy
does or does not promise.

## What happens in practice

Less than that, often.

When Cozi was acquired by OurFamilyWizard — announced 4 May 2022 — the press
release described the deal, the market and the leadership. It said nothing about
user data, the privacy policy, or what would happen to accounts. We could find no
contemporaneous public statement from either company on data handling at the
time.

That is not unusual and it is not evidence of wrongdoing. It is simply what
acquisition announcements are: documents written for the industry press, not for
the people whose data is in the boxes.

## Three cases where it became a public issue

Worth knowing, because they show what the guardrails look like when they are
tested.

**23andMe, 2025.** When the genetic-testing company entered bankruptcy, the
chairman of the US Federal Trade Commission wrote to the bankruptcy trustee,
quoting the company's own policy allowing data to be "accessed, sold or
transferred" in a sale, and stating that any purchaser should expressly agree to
be bound by the existing privacy commitments.

**Kurbo by WW, 2022.** The FTC fined the operator of a weight-management app used
by children as young as eight, and ordered it to delete the children's personal
information **and destroy the algorithms derived from it**.

**Edmodo, 2023.** An education platform was ordered to delete models and
algorithms built from children's data collected without parental consent — and
the order was written to bind the company even though it had already suspended
US operations.

The through-line: data about children attracts the most regulatory attention,
and "the company stopped operating" is not a way out of the obligations.

## What to actually check, in two minutes

Open your family app's privacy policy on your phone and search it for
**"merger"** or **"acquisition"**.

**Is there a clause?** Almost certainly yes. That on its own is normal.

**Does it promise notice before the transfer?** This is the real question, and
most do not.

**Does it say what happens to children's data specifically?** In an app built
for families, silence here is telling.

**Is there an export?** The practical protection. If you can take a copy
whenever you like, a change of owner is a decision you get to make rather than
one made for you.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can a company really sell my family's data?**
In an acquisition, data typically transfers to the buyer as part of the
business, which is different from selling data to advertisers. The clause in
your policy almost certainly permits it. What varies is whether you are told
first, and what the new owner commits to.

**Can I stop it?**
Not usually — but you can leave. If the new arrangement is not one you accept,
export your data, delete the account and go elsewhere. Which is exactly why "can
I get my data out" is worth checking before you need it.

**Does it matter more because it is children's data?**
Yes. Children's data attracts specific protections and, on the evidence of the
enforcement cases above, the most regulatory attention. An app built for
families that says nothing about children in its acquisition clause has told you
something.
