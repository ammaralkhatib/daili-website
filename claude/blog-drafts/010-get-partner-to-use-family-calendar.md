# How to Get Your Partner to Actually Use the Family Calendar

You set up the shared calendar. You put everything in it. Your partner has the
app installed and opens it roughly never.

So you keep answering. "Are we free Saturday?" "What time is the party?" "Did
you book the dentist?" You answer from memory, quickly, because it is easier
than saying "look it up".

That last sentence is the whole problem, and it is the only one you control.

## Why "just check the calendar" fails

Your partner is not choosing between the calendar and chaos. They are choosing
between the calendar and **you** — and you are faster, more accurate, and never
need to be unlocked.

As long as asking you works, opening an app is the slower option. No amount of
reminding changes that arithmetic. Neither does a better app.

Everything below is a way of changing the arithmetic rather than changing their
mind.

## 1. Stop being the interface

This is the one that works, and it is uncomfortable for about two weeks.

When you are asked something that is in the calendar, say "it's in the calendar"
— and then do not follow it with the answer. Not a hint, not "it's on Saturday,
but check". Just the sentence.

You will feel unhelpful. You are not being unhelpful; you are declining to be a
lookup service, which is a job you never applied for.

Two things make it land better. Say once, in advance, that you are going to do
this and why — surprising someone with a new rule reads as a punishment. And be
consistent for a fortnight, because inconsistency teaches them that persistence
works.

## 2. Give them one category they own outright

Being a user of someone else's system is unmotivating. Being responsible for
part of it is different.

Hand over one whole area — football and sports, or medical appointments, or
anything to do with one child. Not "please add things sometimes". A named
category where they are the person who knows, and where you genuinely stop
tracking it.

That last part is what makes it work, and it is the hard bit. If you keep a
mental backup of their category, they will feel it, and the category is not
really theirs.

## 3. Let one real consequence land

Nothing teaches a calendar's value like a small thing going wrong without it.

Pick something survivable. A missed swimming lesson. Turning up in the wrong
clothes. Being the parent who did not know it was own-clothes day. Not a medical
appointment, not a flight, not anything a child would be hurt or embarrassed by.

Then do not rescue it. The rescue is what keeps the system running on you.

One honest caveat: this only works if the response is "ah, I should check the
calendar" and not anger at you for not reminding them. If it is the second
thing, you are not having a calendar problem, and no app in the world fixes
that one.

## 4. Fewer notifications, and aimed at them

Most people who "won't use the app" muted it in week one, because it pinged
about everything.

Fix that properly. Turn off notifications for events they are not responsible
for. Turn off "someone added an event". What should remain is a reminder for
things they personally have to do, and almost nothing else.

An app that only speaks when it has something for you is an app worth having
unmuted. An app that chatters is an app that gets silenced and then forgotten.

## 5. Make entering it faster than telling you

Adding an event has to take under thirty seconds or it will not happen at the
moment somebody says yes to something.

Practical version: set it up so they can add an event from their phone's home
screen or a widget, without hunting for the app. Agree that a rough entry beats
no entry — "party, Sat, ask Emma for address" is a fine calendar item. A
half-filled event that exists is worth more than a perfect one that does not.

If your app makes adding an event a five-screen process, that is a real reason
people do not use it, and it is worth switching for.

## 6. The ten-minute Sunday check-in

Ten minutes, once a week, looking at the next fortnight together.

This is the habit that every family who makes shared calendars work has in some
form, and it does two jobs. It catches the collisions. And it makes the calendar
a thing you both look at, rather than a thing you maintain and they ignore.

Keep it short and unceremonious. Kitchen, phones out, two weeks, three
questions: what is happening that we have not noticed, where do two things
collide, what is missing.

## When it is not really about the calendar

Sometimes none of this works, and it is worth naming why.

If one person is doing all the scheduling, all the remembering and all the
booking, the calendar is not the problem — it is where the problem is visible.
The load was already unequal; the app just made it countable.

That is a conversation about how the household is run, not about software. It is
a better conversation to have than another round of "please just check the app",
and a shared calendar is actually a useful way to start it, because it turns a
feeling into a list you can both look at.

## Frequently asked questions

**What if they genuinely will not engage at all?**
Then stop investing effort in adoption and use the calendar for yourself. It
still reduces your own load. Keep one shared list — usually shopping — because
that one has an immediate payoff for everyone, and leave the rest. A calendar
one person actually uses beats a shared one nobody does.

**Is it fair to let something be missed?**
Only if it is small and no child bears the cost. A missed swimming lesson is a
lesson. A missed hospital appointment is not a teaching tool, it is a
consequence for someone who did not choose any of this. Keep the stakes on the
adults.

**Does a wall calendar work better for some people?**
Often, yes. A paper calendar in the kitchen is visible without anyone deciding
to look, which is its enormous advantage. Plenty of households run both — paper
for ambient awareness, the app for the parent who is not in the kitchen. That is
not a failure, it is a sensible split.
