# What to Look For in a Family App Before You Switch

Every family app's website shows you the same things: a calendar, lists, some
smiling people. The feature lists are nearly identical, which is why choosing
between them from the websites is close to impossible.

The differences that matter are the ones you find out in month three, after
you have moved your household in and it is expensive to leave. These twelve
questions surface most of them in about twenty minutes.

Do this before you migrate, not after.

## The five that decide whether it will work at all

**1. Does everyone in the house have a device it runs on properly?**
Not "is there an app" — is it a first-class app on both platforms? Plenty of
family tools are excellent on one and an afterthought on the other. If half
your household is on the weaker one, that half will not use it.

**2. Is there a limit on how far ahead you can see?**
Some free tiers cap the calendar horizon. This sounds minor until March, when
you cannot see the summer holiday you need to book leave for. Test it directly:
add an event six months out on day one.

**3. Can everyone add things, or only some people?**
Read-only members are the quiet killer. If one adult can only view, you are
still the household's only scheduler and nothing has changed.

**4. What do children get?**
Can a child have their own access without their own email address? At what age
does the app want them to have a real account? Can a teenager mark something
private? If you have children between about 10 and 16, these answers decide
whether the app survives their adolescence.

**5. How many taps to add an event?**
Count them. If adding a thing takes more than about thirty seconds, it will not
happen at the moment someone says yes to a party invitation — and an event
nobody entered is the whole problem you are trying to solve.

## The four about money

**6. What is actually free, and for how long?**
Not "is there a free tier" but which of the things you need are in it. Ads?
A limit on members, lists or events? Free trials that quietly become
subscriptions?

**7. What does it cost per family, not per person?**
Some apps charge per user, which for a family of five is a completely different
number from the one on the pricing page.

**8. If you stop paying, what happens?**
Does the app become read-only, lock the calendar, or delete anything? This is
rarely on the pricing page and is usually in the terms.

**9. Who pays for the free tier?**
If there is no paid option at all, advertising is the model, and the product
holding your children's schedule is funded by profiling. That may be an
acceptable trade. It should be a decision.

## The three nobody asks until it is too late

**10. Can you get your data out?**
Look for an export or download in the settings — not a policy promising one on
request. The difference between a button and an email-and-wait is the difference
between leaving in ten minutes and leaving in a month.

**11. Where is the data stored, and who is the company?**
A country name in the privacy policy. An identifiable company with an address.
If an app holding your family's schedule will not say who or where it is, that
is the answer.

**12. What happens if the company is sold?**
Check the privacy policy for the merger or acquisition clause. Almost all of
them reserve the right to transfer your data to a buyer. The question is
whether the policy promises to *tell you* first — most do not.

## The twenty-minute test

Do not read more reviews. Install the top candidate and run this:

**Minute 1–5.** Add an event six months out. Add an event with an end time and
a place. Invite a second adult and check they can add something, not just see
it.

**Minute 5–10.** Open the settings. Find the export. Find delete-account. Note
whether either exists.

**Minute 10–15.** Open the privacy policy on a phone and search it for:
*server*, *advertis*, *child*, *merger*, *delete*. Five searches.

**Minute 15–20.** Add three shopping list items and send the list to someone
else in the house. Have them add one. If that round trip is awkward, everything
else will be too.

A family app that survives those twenty minutes is very likely to survive month
three. One that does not will not improve later.

## The one thing the feature list cannot tell you

Whether your family will use it.

That is not a property of the app. It is a property of how you introduce it, who
agreed to it, and whether anything in the house actually depends on it. The best
app in the world, announced rather than agreed, will be abandoned in a fortnight
— and a mediocre one everybody uses beats it completely.

So budget your effort accordingly. Twenty minutes choosing. Rather more than
that on the introduction.

## Frequently asked questions

**How many apps should I trial?**
Two, at most. Trialling five means your family is asked to learn five things and
commits to none. Shortlist on the twelve questions, install the best one, and
give it two weeks.

**Should I pay before I know it works?**
If a month's subscription is what unlocks the thing you actually need — usually
the calendar horizon — then yes, pay for one month. It is cheaper than picking
wrong and moving twice.

**Is it worth switching at all if the current app mostly works?**
Often not. Switching costs a family real effort and some goodwill. Switch when
something is genuinely blocked — a person who cannot participate, a limit you
keep hitting, or a privacy position you are not comfortable with — not because
another app has a nicer screenshot.
