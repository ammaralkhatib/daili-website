# What to Leave with a Babysitter or Grandparent for a Weekend Away

There is a version of this that takes forty minutes and produces four pages
nobody reads. There is another that takes ten minutes and produces one page that
gets used.

This is the second one.

The test for every line: **would a calm, capable adult who does not live here
need this at 8pm on Saturday?** If yes, it goes on the page. If it is something
they would work out, or something you are including to feel prepared, leave it
off. A sitter who has to search four pages for a phone number is worse off than
one with a short list they have actually read.

## The one-page rule

One side of A4, or one note on the fridge. That is the whole handover.

It works because a sitter reads it once, at the start, and then glances at it
under pressure. Anything past one page gets skimmed at the start and forgotten at
the moment it matters.

Everything below fits on that page. If yours does not, you have included things
that belong in conversation instead.

## Contacts — in the order they should be called

Not an address book. A sequence.

1. **You**, and the other parent, with the note "if no answer, try the next"
2. **One other adult who is nearby** — a neighbour, a relative in the same town.
   Someone who can physically arrive
3. **The GP or family doctor**, with the out-of-hours number if that is
   different
4. **The local emergency number**, written out. Obvious to you; not obvious to a
   sixteen-year-old sitter in a country they did not grow up in

Say plainly what "can't reach you" means. "We are at a wedding, phones on
silent, try both of us then call Anna next door" is worth more than three extra
numbers.

## Medical — the part that actually matters

This is the section that justifies the whole page.

- **Allergies**, and what happens. "Nuts — swells, use the pen in the green bag,
  then call an ambulance" is usable. "Nut allergy" is not
- **Current medication**, with the dose and the time. Written, not remembered
- **Anything given regularly for a condition** — where it is kept and how it is
  given
- **Health insurance details**, or the card itself
- **The GP's name and the practice address**

Two things people forget. Write down **what a child is allowed for ordinary
things**: paracetamol, ibuprofen, a plaster, the dose and the last time it was
given. And write **what has already been given today**, because a sitter who
does not know that is a sitter who cannot safely give anything.

## The consent question

For an evening out, nothing formal is needed. For a weekend, and especially
where the carer is not a parent, it is worth leaving a short written note saying
who is caring for your children, over what dates, and that they may seek medical
treatment if it is needed.

Whether such a letter carries legal weight varies a lot by country, and a
hospital's own policy may matter more than the letter. It is still worth having:
in practice it removes a conversation at the desk at the exact moment nobody
wants one.

*General information, not legal advice — rules differ by country.*

## Routines that prevent a 9pm phone call

Most calls to parents are not emergencies. They are "she says she's allowed to
watch this" and "he won't go to bed".

Pre-empt the predictable three:

- **Bedtime**, and what happens before it. The bath, the two books, the specific
  cuddly toy that must be found
- **Food** — what they eat, what they refuse, what they are allowed to negotiate
  for. "Yoghurt yes, second yoghurt no"
- **Screens** — how long, what, and whether it is before or after something else

Then one line that is worth more than the other three combined: **"If you are
not sure, say no and tell them we'll decide tomorrow."** It gives the sitter
permission to be boring, which is exactly what you want, and it ends the
negotiation without a phone call.

## House and access

Short and practical.

Where the keys are and how the door locks. The wifi password. The alarm code and
what to do if it goes off. Where the fuse box is. Which neighbour has a spare
key. What to do if the heating stops. And the one thing in every house that is
weird — the tap that has to be turned a certain way, the door that sticks, the
smoke alarm that goes off when you make toast.

That last one is the single most appreciated line on the page.

## What to leave OUT

The instinct with a handover is to include everything. Some things are actively
better left off.

- **Full bank card numbers.** If they may need to buy something, leave cash or a
  low-limit arrangement
- **Passwords beyond the wifi.** Not the tablet's parental control PIN, not your
  email
- **Where the safe is, and what is in it**
- **Original passports and certificates.** A copy if there is a reason. The
  originals stay where they live
- **Anything about a child that is not the sitter's business.** A carer needs to
  know what keeps a child safe and what makes bedtime work. They do not need
  their school reports or family history

A handover sheet often sits on a kitchen counter for a weekend, and other people
come and go. Write it as something that could be seen by a visitor, because it
probably will be.

## Grandparents are a different job

A paid sitter needs instruction. A grandparent needs a different thing: what has
changed.

They raised children, possibly yours. What they do not have is this year's
allergy, this year's bedtime, this school's pickup rule, or the fact that the
five-year-old now refuses food they loved in June. Skip the basics and write the
deltas.

The one thing to be explicit about, kindly: medication. "Please don't give
anything without messaging us first" is a normal sentence and it prevents the
most common well-meant mistake.

## Frequently asked questions

**What does a grandparent need that a paid sitter does not?**
Less instruction and more updates. Grandparents know how to look after children;
what they need is what has changed since they last did it — current allergies,
current bedtime, current rules — plus a clear line about medication.

**How much medical detail is appropriate?**
Enough to act on, no more. Allergies and what to do, current medication with
doses, what has been given today, what is allowed for ordinary aches, and the
GP's number. Not a full history, and nothing about a diagnosis a carer does not
need in order to keep your child safe.

**Do I need all this for one evening out?**
No. For a few hours: your numbers, one nearby adult, allergies and medication,
bedtime, and the wifi. The full page is for overnight and longer, when the
sitter has to make decisions without you.
