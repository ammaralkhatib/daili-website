# 7 Rules for a Shared Family Calendar Everyone Actually Follows

Search for advice on shared family calendars and you get setup tutorials. How
to create the calendar, how to invite people, which app has which feature.

None of that is why family calendars fail. They fail because two weeks in, one
adult is still the only person entering anything, someone gets double-booked
anyway, and quietly everyone goes back to asking each other.

A shared calendar is not a piece of software. It is an agreement about how a
household handles time, and the software just holds it. What follows are the
seven parts of that agreement. Read them, argue about them at the kitchen table,
change the ones that do not fit you, and write down what you land on.

## Rule 1: One calendar, not four

The most common failure is not "nobody uses it". It is that everybody uses a
different one. One parent lives in their work calendar, the other in a paper
diary, the teenager in their phone's default calendar, and the family calendar
is a fifth place that duplicates parts of the other four.

There must be exactly one place where the answer lives. Not one *app* — you can
still keep your work calendar private — but one place that answers the question
"what is happening in this family on Thursday".

Everything else feeds into it or is irrelevant to it. If your work meeting means
you cannot do the school run, that fact belongs in the family calendar. The
meeting itself does not.

## Rule 2: If it is not on the calendar, it did not happen

This is the hardest rule and the one that makes the other six work.

The moment someone can say "but I told you about it", the calendar is no longer
the source of truth — memory is, and memory is exactly what you were trying to
stop relying on. The rule has to bite: if a thing is not on the calendar, nobody
is expected to have planned around it.

That sounds harsh. It is far kinder than the alternative, which is one person
carrying every arrangement in their head and being blamed when they drop one.

Give it a fortnight of grace while everyone learns, then hold it.

## Rule 3: Whoever accepts the invitation adds it

Here is the pattern that quietly kills shared calendars. One person is the
scheduler. Everyone else tells them things, and they type them in. That person
is now a human data-entry service, and the calendar has not reduced their load —
it has formalised it.

The rule that fixes it: **the person who said yes is the person who enters it.**

Your child's friend's mother messages you about a birthday party. You reply
"yes, he'd love to". You add it — not later, not by telling your partner.
Twenty seconds, while you still have the message open.

If someone genuinely cannot do this — a grandparent, a young child — then
somebody enters it on their behalf, out loud, in front of them. It stays a
shared habit rather than one person's job.

## Rule 4: Colours mean people, not categories

Most families start by colouring events by type: blue for school, green for
sport, red for medical.

Try it for a month and you will find you never ask "what colour is sport". You
ask "where does Emma need to be, and who is taking her". Colour by person and
the calendar answers that at a glance: a week that is mostly one colour tells
you instantly that one child, or one parent, is carrying too much.

Give every family member a colour, including the adults. Use one extra colour
for things that belong to the whole household — holidays, the boiler service,
visitors staying.

## Rule 5: Every event needs a place and an end time

An event with only a start time is half an arrangement.

"Football 4pm" does not tell you whether you are free at 5, whether you can
schedule something after it, or whether the other parent can make the dentist.
"Football 4–5.30pm, Riverside pitch, Dad taking" is a plan.

Three fields, every time: **when it ends, where it is, and who is responsible.**
That last one goes in the title, not buried in the notes — you want to see it in
the month view without tapping.

This is also the rule that makes a calendar useful to somebody who is not you.
If a grandparent has to step in on Thursday, an event with a place and an end
time is something they can act on.

## Rule 6: Two weeks ahead, minimum

A calendar you only look at today is a to-do list with dates on it.

The value comes from seeing the collision before it happens — the week where
both parents are away on the same night, the Saturday with three things on it,
the school holiday nobody booked leave for. None of that is visible if you only
ever look at today and tomorrow.

So: two weeks is the minimum working horizon, and school and holiday dates go in
the moment the school publishes them, even if that is eight months out.

Worth checking before you commit to an app: **some free plans limit how far
ahead you can see.** If yours does, you cannot follow this rule, and rule 6 is
the one that catches the expensive mistakes.

## Rule 7: One ten-minute review a week

Every family that makes this work has some version of the same habit: a short,
regular look at the week ahead, together.

Ten minutes. Sunday evening is the common choice. Open the next two weeks, out
loud, and answer three questions.

- What is happening that somebody has not noticed?
- Where do two things collide?
- What is missing that we know about but nobody has entered?

That is the whole meeting. It does not need an agenda, it does not need everyone
sitting down formally, and it is over before anyone gets bored. It is also
where the other six rules get repaired when they slip.

## Put the rules where the family can see them

Rules that live in one person's head are that person's preferences. Rules on the
fridge are the household's.

Write the seven down — or your edited version of them — and stick them up for the
first month. It feels slightly silly. It also ends the argument about whether
someone *should* have added the thing, because the answer is on the wall.

## Frequently asked questions

**What if one person refuses to use it?**
Then you have a relationship conversation, not a calendar problem, and no app
fixes it. What does help: stop reminding them verbally. Every time you relay
what is on the calendar, you prove they do not need to open it. Let one small
thing be missed. That is uncomfortable, and it is usually the only thing that
works.

**How much detail is too much?**
If an event needs scrolling to read, it is too much. When, where, who, and
anything a stand-in would need. Everything else — the address you already know,
the kit list, the group chat context — belongs in a note or nowhere.

**Should children be able to add events?**
Yes, from about ten. A child who can add their own football match is a child who
has some ownership of the family's time, and it is one fewer thing routed
through an adult. Expect a few mistakes and some very optimistic entries. That
is a small price.
