You have done the research. You are quietly excited about this one. Your family is not, because from where they are standing this is the third app in two years and each one arrived as homework.

Their reluctance is reasonable. Every previous attempt cost them effort and then quietly died, which taught them that the sensible response to a new app is to wait it out.

So the goal of a trial is not to get them enthusiastic. It is to ask for so little that agreeing costs them nothing, while still finding out honestly whether it works.

Do not announce it as a system

The worst opening is "I've set us up on a new family app, everyone please download it and add your stuff."

That asks four people to change a habit for a benefit they have not felt, on the authority of someone who is excited. It also frames the whole thing as yours, which means any friction is your fault and quitting is free.

The better opening is smaller and more specific: "I want to try one thing for two weeks — the shopping list. Nothing else changes. If it's annoying we stop."

That is a request nobody has a reason to refuse.

Start with the shopping list, not the calendar

This is the most useful thing in this article.

Almost everyone starts with the calendar, because the calendar is the point. The calendar is also the worst possible starting feature: the payoff is delayed, it only pays off when everyone uses it, and it requires people to enter things about the future, which is exactly the habit they do not have.

The shopping list is the opposite. The payoff is immediate and obvious — someone is standing in a supermarket and the thing they need is on their phone. It works even if only two people use it. And adding to it takes four seconds while looking at an empty cupboard, which requires no planning at all.

A family that has successfully adopted a shared shopping list will adopt a calendar. A family that starts with the calendar frequently never gets to the list.

Set it up so nobody has to do any setup

Do this before you say anything.

Create the family, add everyone, enter the handful of things that make it immediately useful, and turn off almost every notification. Then the invitation they receive leads to something already working rather than an empty screen and a wizard.

Notifications are the specific killer. An app that pings about everything gets muted in week one, and a muted app is invisible. Leave on only what is genuinely for that person, and nothing else. You can always add more later; you cannot un-annoy someone.

Two weeks, one feature, one honest measure

Run it for two weeks, on the one feature, and judge it on one thing only:

Has somebody other than me added something, without being asked?

Not whether people say they like it. Not whether you like it. Somebody else, unprompted, put something in. That is the entire test, because that is the behaviour the whole thing depends on.

If that is happening by the end of week two, the app has a chance. If it is not, adding features will not help — and the honest conclusion may be that the household does not want a shared system right now, which is worth knowing before you spend another three months pushing.

Add the second thing only when the first is boring

The right moment to introduce the calendar is when the shopping list has stopped being a new thing anyone thinks about.

Then do the same as before: you set it up, you enter the term dates and the fixed points, and the ask is small. "The calendar's in there now too, with the school stuff already in it. Have a look when you get a chance."

Not a launch. A thing that is already there.

If it fails, say so out loud

The reason your family is sceptical is that the previous attempts were never formally ended. They just faded, and nobody said anything, and the app stayed on everyone's phone as a small monument to a thing that did not work.

So end it properly. "That didn't work, we're stopping, delete it." Twenty seconds, and it costs you a little pride.

It is worth it, because it is what makes the next attempt possible. A family that has seen you end one cleanly will give the next one a fair try. A family that has watched three apps die quietly on their home screen will not.
