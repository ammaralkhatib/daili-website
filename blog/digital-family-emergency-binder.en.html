The emergency binder is good advice. Print the documents, put them in a folder, keep the folder somewhere you can grab it on the way out. Germany's civil protection office has recommended a Dokumentenmappe for decades, and the US version — FEMA's Emergency Financial First Aid Kit — says much the same thing.

It has one flaw, and it is the same flaw every time: the binder is at home.

Fires and floods are the emergencies the paper binder is built for, and it handles those well. But the emergencies families actually have are smaller and more frequent. A child gets hurt at school. A parent is admitted and someone asks about their medication. You are at the airport and the check-in desk wants a document number. In every one of those, the binder is in a cupboard on the other side of town.

So build both. The paper binder protects the originals. A digital version — six or seven files on the phone in your pocket — covers everything else. This is how to build the second one so it is still useful in two years.

Which documents need the original, and which do not

Start here, because it decides everything else. Not every document needs the same treatment, and the German civil protection office (BBK) sorts them into three tiers that are worth borrowing whatever country you live in.

Originals only — a copy will not do.

Family certificates: birth, marriage and death certificates, or the family record book. These prove legal status and authorities want the real thing.

Original or a certified copy.

Financial and legal papers: savings and account contracts, securities, insurance policies, pension statements, tax assessments, qualifications and diplomas, rental and leasing contracts, wills, advance directives and powers of attorney.

A simple copy is fine.

Identity documents, passports, driving licence, vehicle registration, vaccination records, property records, employment and benefits paperwork, insurance payment receipts, memberships.

That third tier is your digital binder. Those are the documents where a photograph on your phone genuinely does the job — and they are, not by coincidence, the ones you need at short notice. The first two tiers stay in the safe, and a copy of them lives in the digital binder only so you can quote a number, not so you can present it.

The five sections

Keep it to five. A digital binder that mirrors a filing cabinet will not be searched under pressure.

1. Identity. Passport or ID page for every family member, plus birth certificates. The photo page only — nobody needs the visa stamps.

2. Medical. Health insurance cards, vaccination records, and a single page per person listing allergies, current medication with doses, and the GP's and dentist's phone numbers. That one-page summary is the single most-used item in the whole binder.

3. Money and home. Insurance policy numbers with the claims phone number, the tenancy agreement or deed, and a list of bank accounts — account numbers and the bank's phone number, never passwords or full card numbers.

4. Permissions. Consent or custody letters, powers of attorney, advance directives. Anything that lets someone else act for you or your child.

5. Contacts. Two emergency contacts outside your household, the school and nursery, the vet, and your building's emergency plumber or electrician. It sounds mundane until the pipe bursts on a Sunday.

What to photograph, and what to scan

Photograph the tier-three documents. A clean phone photo, flat, in daylight, no shadow across the text. That is enough for a hospital desk or an airline agent.

Scan as PDF anything with more than one page or anything you might need to send on — the tenancy agreement, an insurance policy, a consent letter. Most phones now scan to PDF from the camera app or the notes app, no extra software needed.

Two things not to do. Do not photograph a document at an angle in bad light and assume you will re-take it later; you will not. And do not put anything in the binder that is useful to a thief on its own — full card numbers, passwords, a signed blank form. A copy of a passport is of limited use to a stranger. A photo of a card front and back is not.

Naming and folders that survive panic

The binder is only worth building if you can find one file in it while someone is talking to you. Two rules do most of the work.

Name every file who-what. emma-vaccinations, emma-insurance-card, ammar-passport. Not IMG_4471. Not Scan 12 Feb. When you are searching under stress you know the person and you know the thing, and that is all you will type.

One folder per person, plus one called household. That is the whole structure. Resist adding a folder for years, or one for "old". Old documents get replaced, not archived — a binder with two versions of the same insurance card is a binder that will hand you the expired one.

Who else needs access, and how much

This is the step people skip, and it is the one that matters if you are the person in the ambulance rather than the one holding the phone.

At least one other adult should be able to reach the medical section without you. That does not mean sharing everything: a partner needs the whole binder, a grandparent looking after the children for a weekend needs the medical page and the consent letter and nothing else, and a neighbour who might collect a child from school needs a phone number and a name.

The BBK's own advice is to keep copies somewhere other than your home too — with relatives, a notary, or in a bank box. Doing that digitally is faster, but the principle is the same: one location is not a backup.

The twenty-minute annual refresh

Any system that needs monthly maintenance is abandoned by March. This one needs twenty minutes a year.

Pick a date you already remember — the start of the school year works well — and do three things. Replace anything that expired or changed: a renewed passport, a new insurance card, a medication that has been stopped. Add anyone new. Then check that the other adult can still open it, on their own device, without asking you how.

Put the date in the calendar as a repeating event while you are building the binder. It takes ten seconds now and it is the only reason the binder will still be true next year.

Why offline is the whole point

An emergency binder that needs a login and a signal is not an emergency binder.

Think about where you will actually open it: a hospital corridor, an underground car park, a school office with terrible reception, a foreign country where your data has stopped working. Cloud storage that downloads a file on demand fails in every one of those.

Whatever you use, check one thing before you trust it. Put your phone in airplane mode and try to open your child's vaccination record. If it appears, you have a digital emergency binder. If you get a spinner, you have a folder in the cloud — which is worth having, but it is not this.
