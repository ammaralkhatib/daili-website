Every family app has a free tier, and every free tier is missing something. The question is never "is it free" — it is which specific thing is missing, and whether that thing is the one your household needs.

This is a guide to the patterns rather than a price table, because prices change and the patterns do not. Once you can recognise which shape of free tier you are looking at, you can work out in five minutes whether it will frustrate you.

The five things usually held back

1. How far ahead you can see. The most consequential and the least obvious. Some free tiers cap the calendar horizon at a month. Everything feels fine until the moment you try to plan the summer.

2. The month view itself, usually on mobile. You get an agenda list; the grid is paid. Whether that matters is personal — some people think in lists, most parents think in weeks.

3. Reminders. Often one per event on free, several on paid. If you need a reminder the night before and an hour before, that is a paid feature in a surprising number of apps.

4. Search. Being unable to search your own calendar sounds trivial until you are trying to remember when the boiler was last serviced.

5. An ad-free experience. Which brings us to the real question.

Ads are the price, and it is worth naming

If an app is free and has no paid tier at all, advertising is the business model. If it is free with a paid tier, the free version is usually ad-supported and the paid one is not — "no ads" is one of the most commonly sold upgrades in this category.

For most apps this is an ordinary trade and not worth agonising over. A family organiser is a slightly different case, because of what it knows: your children's names and ages, which school they attend, what time they finish, which afternoons nobody is home, and often where you are.

That does not mean any particular app is misusing it. It does mean that "free" has a price, the price is an advertising relationship built around your family's routine, and you should decide about it on purpose rather than by default.

What free tiers rarely take away

Worth saying, because the alarmist version of this article would not.

Free tiers almost never limit how many people are in your family, or how many events you can create, or take away the shared shopping list — which for many households is the single most-used feature and the one with the most immediate daily payoff.

So a free tier is genuinely usable for a lot of families. The households it frustrates are specific: the ones that plan more than a month ahead, and the ones who do not want an ad network in the middle of their week.

The four questions that settle it

Do you plan further ahead than a month? If your family has school-age children, you almost certainly do, and a horizon cap will keep hurting.

Does more than one adult need to add things? If a free tier makes anyone read-only, it does not solve the problem you have.

Do you mind ads on your family's schedule? A real question with a real answer either way.

Would you rather pay once for the household, or per person? Per-family pricing at €20–40 a year is a different proposition from per-seat pricing across five people. Check which one you are looking at — the pricing page usually shows one number and does not always say which.

When paying is the right answer

Here is the thing most comparison articles will not say, because they are trying to move you to a different app.

If your current app works and one limit is the problem, pay for it. A yearly subscription that removes the limit costs less than the effort of moving a family. Switching apps means re-teaching four people a new habit, losing your lists, and spending a fortnight where nobody trusts the calendar. That is worth more than €39.

Switch when something is structurally wrong, not when something is annoying. Someone in the household cannot participate at all. The company will not say where your data lives or cannot give it back. You are not comfortable with the funding model. Those are reasons to move. "I cannot see August in March" is a reason to pay.

The honest test before you pay

Pay for one month rather than a year, and use it properly for those four weeks. Most annual subscriptions in this category are a decent saving and a genuine commitment; a month tells you whether the thing you thought was blocking you was really the thing.

If the app is only sold annually, check the refund terms before you buy, not after.
