It happens more often than people expect. A small app runs out of money, a bigger one buys a competitor and folds it in, or a service quietly stops being updated until it stops working.

If you have had an email like that, the useful thing is to know the order to do things in — because there is usually a deadline, and the parts people rescue first are rarely the parts they miss most.

First: how long do you actually have?

Read the notice carefully for two different dates, because they are often not the same.

The date the service stops working. After this the app stops syncing, or stops opening.

The date the data is deleted. Sometimes weeks or months later. Sometimes the same day.

If the notice does not say when data is deleted, assume it is the shutdown date and work to that. If it gives you a data-download window, put the deadline in your calendar right now — this is the most commonly missed deadline in the whole process, because the shutdown date is the one that gets the headline.

The order to rescue things

Work down this list. It is ordered by how hard each thing is to reconstruct.

1. Anything only that app knows. Not the school calendar, which the school republishes. The things that exist nowhere else: your own notes on events, the family recipes with your annotations, documents you uploaded, photographs attached to events. These are irreplaceable and people rescue them last.

2. Future events. Everything from today forwards. If there is a calendar export or feed, take it. If not, screenshot the month views — ugly, fast, adequate.

3. Recurring commitments. Weekly clubs, monthly bills, annual renewals. Easy to forget precisely because they repeat: you never think about them, so you do not notice they are gone until the week they do not happen.

4. Birthdays and anniversaries, with years. These often export badly, losing the birth year. Check them, and write down the years separately if you have to.

5. Lists. Shopping and to-do lists, but only the live items.

6. Past events. Almost nobody needs these. Skip unless there is a specific reason.

Use the export if there is one, and ask if there is not

If the app has an export or download, use it today rather than on the last day. Download servers get busy at the end and support queues stop being answered.

If there is no export button, and you are in the EU or UK, you can ask. Data portability gives you the right to receive your personal data in a structured, commonly used, machine-readable format, and companies generally have a month to respond. A company that is winding down may be slow or unresponsive — so send it early, and treat it as a bonus rather than the plan.

The thing most people forget

The dates that live in other people's heads.

A family calendar is not just a record — it is the shared memory of the household. When it disappears, what goes with it is all the arrangements that were entered once and never spoken about again: whose parent has which grandchild on which weekend, when the car service is due, which Thursday the bins change.

Before the app goes dark, sit down with the other adults for fifteen minutes with the next three months open on screen, and read it out loud. You will find three or four things that nobody else knew about. Those are the ones that would otherwise have been discovered by missing them.

Then choose the next one carefully

There is a temptation to grab whatever is most convenient in the moment, especially under a deadline. That is how households end up switching twice in a year.

You have at least a few days. Spend twenty minutes of it on the questions that actually matter — whether everyone can add things, how far ahead you can see, whether you can get your data out of this one — so that the next shutdown notice, if it ever comes, is much less painful.

And check one thing you would not have thought to check before: does the new app let you export your own data whenever you want? Having just lived through this, that question has stopped being theoretical.
