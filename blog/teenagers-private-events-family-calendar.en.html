A shared family calendar works beautifully until somebody turns thirteen.

Up to then, everything a child does is a logistics problem for an adult, so everything goes in. Then one day your daughter does not want the whole family to see who she is meeting on Friday, and you have to decide something you have never had to decide before.

The instinct is to treat it as a rule question — allowed or not allowed. It is better treated as a boundary question, and boundaries work better when they are agreed once rather than renegotiated every Friday.

What you need to know versus what you want to know

Be honest about the difference, because it is the whole argument.

What you need is what makes the household work and what keeps them safe. Where they will be, roughly. When they will be home. How they are getting there. Whether they need a lift, money, or dinner kept.

What you want is often more: who exactly they are meeting, what they will be doing, why they seemed quiet about it.

The second list is not illegitimate — you are their parent and you care. But it is not the same as the first, and a family calendar is a logistics tool. When it starts carrying the second list, it stops being a calendar and becomes surveillance, and teenagers detect that instantly.

The three tiers

A model that survives contact with an actual sixteen-year-old:

Must share — non-negotiable. Anything that needs an adult to do something: lifts, money, being collected, being fed, being let in late. Plus a rough location and a return time for anything outside the ordinary. This is not about trust, it is about somebody knowing where you are, which applies to adults too.

Should share — the household's business. Things that occupy a slot other people might want: the family car, the kitchen if friends are coming, a Saturday that was going to be used for something else. Being in a family means your time is partly a shared resource, which is a fair thing to say out loud.

Theirs. Who exactly they are with. What they talked about. The detail of their social life. A private event that says "busy, 6–10pm, town" satisfies every legitimate need in tier one without opening tier three.

That last sentence is the whole answer, and it is worth saying to them in those words: I need to know you are busy and roughly where. I do not need to know who.

What monitoring actually costs you

The practical argument against demanding full visibility is not philosophical. It is that it does not work.

A teenager who feels tracked routes around the tracker. They stop putting things in the calendar, or they put in vague or false entries, and now your calendar is worse than it was — you have less information than before, and no idea which entries to believe.

The version that survives is the one they can live with. A calendar with "busy, 6–10pm" in it is infinitely more useful than an empty calendar and a teenager who tells you nothing.

Setting it up so the argument happens once

Most family apps let an event be visible only to its creator, or shared with some people and not others. If yours does, use it — it turns a repeated negotiation into a setting.

The practical setup:

  • Give them their own account, not a profile you control. At some point in their teens they need to be a person in the system rather than an item in it.
  • Agree that private events are allowed, and that a private event still has to show a time block so the calendar stays useful for planning.
  • Agree what still has to be visible: return times, lifts, anything needing money or a car.
  • Agree that you will not go looking for what is behind a private block, and then do not. The rule is worth nothing if it is not kept.

Worth knowing if you are choosing an app for this: in much of Europe a young person can manage their own account from a country-specific age — 13 in many places, 14 in Austria, 15 in France, 16 in Germany, Ireland and the Netherlands among others. Below that, accounts generally need to be set up with parental consent. It is worth checking before you promise a twelve-year-old their own login.

The conversation, roughly

It goes better if it is short, planned, and not held during an argument about a specific Friday.

Something like: "You are old enough that not everything you do is family business. Here is what I want: if you are out, I want a time block on the calendar and roughly where. It can just say busy. I am not going to ask who, and I am not going to go digging. What I do need to know is when you will be back and whether you need a lift."

Then stop talking and let them respond. The offer is real autonomy in exchange for real logistics, and most teenagers take that deal because it is a good one.

Age by age

Around 12. Usually still fully shared, and usually fine — most of what they do still requires an adult. This is a good year to start asking rather than assuming.

Around 15. The tiers should be in place. Private events with visible time blocks are the normal arrangement. Expect to be tested once or twice; the point of having agreed the rule is that you can point at it rather than improvise.

Around 17. Closer to how you would treat a flatmate. Where they will be and when they are back, because that is what people who live together tell each other. The rest is theirs.

When to change the rules

Two situations justify revisiting, and it is worth naming them in advance so it is not experienced as a punishment.

If something goes genuinely wrong — safety, not disappointment — the arrangement gets reviewed. And if the logistics stop working, if time blocks stop appearing and nobody knows anything, the deal was not kept and it gets renegotiated.

Both are reasonable and both should be said at the start.
