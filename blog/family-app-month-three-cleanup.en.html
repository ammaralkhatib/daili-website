Every review of every family app is written in week one, when everything is clean and the calendar has eleven events in it.

Nobody writes about month three. Month three is when the to-do list has two hundred items, forty of which are done, sixty of which are nobody's, and the rest are things somebody once thought about. It is when people quietly stop opening the app — not because it stopped working, but because looking at it feels bad.

This is a fixable state, and it takes about thirty minutes.

What decay actually looks like

You can recognise it by four symptoms.

Nobody trusts the list. People check with each other instead of checking the app, because the app might be out of date and asking is safer.

There are items with no owner and no date. "Book the thing." "Look into summer camp." These are not tasks, and they never get done, but they sit there making the list longer.

Recurring items are lying. A weekly chore that stopped being weekly in June, a reminder for a class that finished. These are the worst kind, because they generate new false items on their own.

Somebody is doing a private parallel system. A notes app, a paper list, a message thread. That is the clearest sign of all — someone stopped believing the shared system and built their own.

The thirty-minute reset

Do it once, do it with somebody else, and do not do it in stages.

Minutes 0–10: delete completed and dead items. Anything done, anything about an event that has passed, anything nobody can now explain. Do not read them carefully. If it takes more than three seconds to decide, it goes.

Minutes 10–20: fix or kill everything without an owner. Go through what is left and give each item a person and a rough date. Anything you cannot assign to someone, out loud, right now — delete it. It was a thought, not a task.

Minutes 20–25: audit the recurring items. These are the ones that regenerate. Look at every repeating task and reminder and ask whether it is still true. Most families find two or three that stopped being true months ago.

Minutes 25–30: fix the notifications. Decay is usually accompanied by muted notifications. Turn most of them off deliberately, and leave only reminders for things the person is personally responsible for. An app that only speaks when it matters gets unmuted.

The rule that prevents most of it

One sentence, worth agreeing as a household:

A to-do with no owner and no date is a wish.

Wishes are allowed — but they go somewhere else. A "someday" list, a note, a conversation. Not in the list that is supposed to tell people what to do this week.

This single rule is the difference between a list that stays at fifteen items and one that reaches two hundred.

Archive, do not delete, when it matters

One caveat to all the deleting above.

Some things look dead and are not: the annual ones. The boiler service, the insurance renewal, the passport that expires in three years, the thing you do once each August. Deleting those means re-inventing them next year.

If your app has an archive or a "someday" list, put them there. If it does not, move them into the calendar as a future event rather than leaving them in a to-do list where they clutter every week for eleven months.

The four habits that stop it coming back

One ten-minute sweep a month. Same slot each time. Delete what is done, reassign what has drifted. Ten minutes monthly is much less painful than thirty minutes quarterly, and it never gets to the point where the list feels hopeless.

Items get an owner at the moment they are created. Not later. Later never comes.

Check recurring items twice a year. Start of school year, start of calendar year. Two minutes each time.

When somebody starts a private list, ask why. It is the earliest warning sign, and it is always about something specific — the shared list is too noisy, or their things get lost in it, or they do not trust it. That is fixable information, not a betrayal.

When to delete a whole list and start again

Sometimes the reset is not worth it.

If a list has more than about a hundred items and nobody can say what most of them are, the honest move is to delete it entirely and rebuild from what people actually remember. What is genuinely important will be back within a week, because somebody will need it. What does not come back was not important.

That feels reckless. It is much less reckless than keeping a list that everyone has already stopped believing.
